If there is an AI economic shock, what kind would it be?

Five explanations, each with evidence and a test that would prove it wrong. Ranked by how well current data supports them. These are hypotheses for SPAR to test, not findings.

Working view. Through 2030 the evidence points more to a preparedness shock than a speed shock. Adoption inside banks is unplanned (pilots without governance or redesign) and lands on people entering the labour market. It would arrive as quiet adjustment: leavers not replaced, fewer entry-level hires, more informal work. Open layoffs and a jump in the unemployment rate are the less likely signs. An external financial bust is the exception: it could hit without adoption and is the least measured.

Five Moody's scenarios, three Indonesian speeds

Moody's Analytics gives five US scenarios with probabilities (the rounded average of seven economists and an AI model). We map them to Slow, Medium and Extreme, flag where they do not fit Indonesia, and let you set your own probabilities.

Source: Moody's Analytics, The Macroeconomic Consequences of AI (Redux), August 2026, endnote 3 and scenario sections. US figures; probabilities are subjective, not estimated. Bars show the range across authors.

Set the probabilities for Indonesia

Weights are normalised to 100%. The Indonesia prior is our judgement: 5 points moved out of Extreme and 10 out of Medium into Slow, because usage is 14.8% against 33.0% and Extreme needs adoption to rise from 18% to 85% in two years. Replace it with expert elicitation.

Where we adjusted Moody's, and why

Moody's models the United States. These are the points where Indonesian evidence made us change or drop something.

Does not fit us. The stock-wealth channel (two-thirds of AI's contribution to US growth in 2025), the US labour supply story, unemployment as the shock gauge, and the assumption that gains stay with domestic firms. We left these out or replaced them with a flagged gap rather than invent numbers.

Caveats, sources and what to feed next

Caveats

  • Stress test, not a forecast. Moody's probabilities are US economists' judgement; ours are a prior to be replaced.
  • Attrition is the model's biggest lever, not adoption: all reported leavers (6.11% weighted) gives 602 dismissals in the extreme case, voluntary-only leavers (4.38%) gives 7,718. Both bases are published; neither is the answer.
  • Exposed share is 0.582, measured from 20,088 Indonesian banking ads — a task-based score transferred by title, not observed displacement.
  • Bank panel: eight banks, 218,129 staff, all December 2025 — about 13% of finance and insurance employment, not the sector.
  • ID-05 shares are of 91 respondents from 35 institutions, not of banks.
  • Several inputs are placeholders: matching parameters, destination split, pass-through phi, JKP acceptance rate, wage.
  • Business adoption (AWS/Strand) and the Anthropic index are secondary; indicators ID-12, ID-14 and ID-20 are pending review.
  • Page and workbook do not sync automatically; model.js mirrors the Sheet.

    No one publishes fill status

    Not the six channels we cannot reach, and not the ones we can. No Indonesian board exposes a close-with-hire flag, so an advertisement that disappears is indistinguishable from one that was filled. Pasker ID publishes aggregate placements, not vacancy outcomes.

    That is the measurement this whole study waits on: a posting measures what an employer published. It never says anyone was hired. Every postings figure here is demand-side, and the trigger ladder's Watch rung sits where it does because of it.

    BPS, by contrast, has a working door: webapi.bps.go.id answers 400 (no key), not 403. A free self-service key lifts every BPS figure from medium to high confidence without a browser.
