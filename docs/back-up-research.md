## 2. M2 — AI deployment and workforce change

### Research backing

Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond (2025), “Generative AI at Work,” *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 140(2), 889–942.

The published study examines AI assistance for 5,172 customer-support agents and reports an average 15% increase in issues resolved per hour, with heterogeneous effects across workers. Humlum and Vestergaard (2026 revision), *Still Waters, Rapid Currents: Early Labor Market Transformation under Generative AI*, NBER Working Paper 33777.

This March 2026 revision supersedes the earlier title *Large Language Models, Small Labor Market Effects*. Linking Danish adoption surveys to administrative employment records, it finds widespread task reorganisation but no average earnings or recorded-hours effects larger than 2% over the studied period. It is particularly useful evidence that work can change before standard labour outcomes move. It remains a working paper, and its institutional setting is Denmark, not Indonesia. [nber](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w33777/w33777.pdf?mc_cid=2bd0264926&mc_eid=976bc2a5b6)

## 4. M4 — Job loss and worker destinations

### Research and institutional backing

Humlum and Vestergaard’s 2026 revision demonstrates why worker destinations matter: occupational switching and task restructuring can occur even when average earnings and hours remain stable. Monitoring only unemployment would miss part of the adjustment process. [nber](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w33777/w33777.pdf?mc_cid=2bd0264926&mc_eid=976bc2a5b6)

Indonesia’s PP No. 6 of 2025, amending the JKP framework, changes eligibility-related provisions, contribution requirements, claim expiry, and evidence of termination. This is direct institutional backing for treating the February 2025 reform as a comparability break in JKP series—not interpreting every increase in claims as increased displacement. [peraturan.bpk.go](https://peraturan.bpk.go.id/Details/314623/pp-no-6-tahun-2025)

### Indicators this supports

- Distinct newly displaced workers, separately from benefit payments or claim cases.
- Applications, approvals, rejections, and reasons for rejection.
- Re-employment status at defined follow-up intervals.
- Formal-to-informal movement.
- Change in occupation, industry, hours, and real earnings.
- Exit from the labour force.
- Benefit exhaustion without a confirmed employment destination.

### Copy-ready justification

> “M4 tracks both job separation and subsequent worker destinations, including re-employment, occupational change, earnings recovery, informality, and labour-force exit. Benefit claims provide a partial administrative signal rather than a complete measure of displacement. Interpretation must account for eligibility, take-up, repeat payments, and changes to programme rules.” [nber](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w33777/w33777.pdf?mc_cid=2bd0264926&mc_eid=976bc2a5b6)

## 5. M5 — Worker support capacity

### Research backing

Card, Kluve and Weber (2018), “What Works? A Meta Analysis of Recent Active Labor Market Program Evaluations,” *Journal of the European Economic Association*, 16(3), 894–931.

The meta-analysis covers more than 200 studies. Average programme impacts are close to zero in the short run but become more positive two to three years after completion, with substantial variation by programme and participant group. This supports measuring outcomes over time rather than treating participation or completion as proof of successful adjustment. [academic.oup](https://academic.oup.com/jeea/article-abstract/16/3/894/4430618)

J-PAL, *Impact Evaluation of Indonesia’s Pre-employment Card (Kartu Prakerja)*.

The evaluation design uses lottery-based programme selection to examine labour outcomes, consumption smoothing, financial behaviour, and take-up. It is a valuable Indonesia-specific methodological reference. However, the project page describes the evaluation design; it should not be cited as a completed positive-impact finding. [povertyactionlab](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/initiative-project/impact-evaluation-indonesias-pre-employment-card-kartu-prakerja)

### Indicators this supports

- Eligible-worker coverage and application take-up.
- Application-to-decision and decision-to-first-payment times.
- Rejection rates and documentation barriers.
- Training demand relative to available places.
- Waiting times, starts, and completion rates.
- Employment and real-earnings outcomes at 3, 6, and 12 months.
- Longer follow-up where feasible, because training benefits may take time.
- Differences by province, sex, age, previous occupation, and worker status.

### Copy-ready justification

> “M5 assesses whether worker-support systems can provide timely income protection, job matching, and training at the scale required. It distinguishes programme inputs and participation from employment and earnings outcomes, recognising that intervention effects vary across groups and may emerge only over longer follow-up periods.” [academic.oup](https://academic.oup.com/jeea/article-abstract/16/3/894/4430618)

### Qualification for your existing indicators

Your workbook correctly warns that PEKA participation is not proof of placement effectiveness, and that JKK return-to-work rehabilitation concerns a different programme and population from JKP beneficiaries. Keep them separate. Neither a training event nor a rehabilitation success rate establishes capacity to absorb displaced bank workers. ## 6. M6 — Consumption and fiscal effects

### Research backing

Ganong and Noel (2019), “Consumer Spending during Unemployment: Positive and Normative Implications,” *American Economic Review*, 109(7), 2383–2424.

Using de-identified bank-account data, the authors show that spending falls sharply when unemployment benefits are exhausted. This provides empirical backing for observing household consumption alongside employment and benefit duration. It is US evidence about unemployment and income support—not an estimate of Indonesia’s AI effects. [aeaweb](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20170537)

Brollo et al. (2024), *Broadening the Gains from Generative AI: The Role of Fiscal Policies*, IMF Staff Discussion Note SDN2024/002.

The note connects AI-driven structural change with social protection, labour-market adjustment, inequality, and tax design. It supports monitoring both expenditure pressures and changes in the tax base. Its modelling is scenario-based and includes a representative advanced-economy calibration; it does not establish an observed Indonesian fiscal shock. [imf](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/sdn/2024/english/sdnea2024002.pdf)

### Indicators this supports

Separate M6 into two subcomponents.

| Component | Candidate indicators | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| M6a — Household transmission | Real consumption per person; food/non-food expenditure; income loss; benefit exhaustion; arrears where accessible | Whether labour-income changes are translating into household stress |
| M6b — Fiscal and social-insurance transmission | Relevant contribution receipts; benefit expenditure; programme caseload and unit cost; labour-related revenue where identifiable | Whether adjustment is changing revenue or support-system financing needs |

### Copy-ready justification

> “M6 monitors whether labour-market adjustment translates into household consumption stress and fiscal or social-insurance pressures. Research on unemployment shows that benefit exhaustion can substantially affect spending, while AI fiscal-policy research identifies potential changes in support needs and tax bases. These are transmission channels to monitor, not evidence that aggregate consumption or revenue changes are caused by AI.” [aeaweb](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20170537)

### Indonesia-specific qualification

Avoid treating national retail sales or tax receipts as sensitive indicators of a relatively small banking-workforce shock. Prefer affected households, occupations, provinces, or employer-linked cohorts where possible.

Also distinguish government budget effects from social-insurance fund effects. A rise in JKP expenditure is not automatically equivalent to the same increase in general-government expenditure.

## What is solid—and what still needs validation

### Literature-backed

You can confidently state that the research supports:

- Separating exposure from adoption and realised outcomes. [ilo](https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-refined-global-index-occupational-exposure)
- Observing task redesign and productivity alongside headcount. [academic.oup](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/140/2/889/7990658)
